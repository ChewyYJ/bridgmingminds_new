# BridgingMinds / MindGarden: demand, audience and route to market

Research date: 1 November 2026. Scope: primary evidence and a testable launch recommendation, not a revenue forecast. The user confirmed **students and early-career adults in Singapore** as the intended audience during this research. Start with adults aged 18+; the global-professional and curious-reader comparisons below are alternatives, not simultaneous launch markets.

## Recommendation

**Initial hypothesis:** recruit Singapore-based adults who have a real discussion, presentation or interview in the next month and want to make their own reasoning clearer. Start with two separate cohorts: tertiary students approaching internships/work, and professionals in their first three years at work. Test the same core learning loop against their different situations before choosing a paying segment.

The strongest proposed promise is **“Practise explaining your ideas before the conversation that matters.”** The product should help someone state a position, support it, respond to a challenge and revise in their own words. This fits the app's short responses, reflection, guiding questions, linked revisions and friend debate. It also creates a bridge from reading an idea to explaining it. A garden is an optional reason to enjoy returning; it is not evidence of learning or a defensible market position by itself.

Do not lead with “another AI interview coach”, a generic critical-thinking score, or the claim that Singaporeans are poor communicators. The evidence supports the importance of these skills and a competitive practice market; it does **not** establish a national skill deficit, an unmet need for this particular app, or willingness to pay for it.

The most practical first route is a small, opt-in, facilitated pilot through a student society, internship/alumni community or independent career coach. A campus careers department is a useful research partner and possible later buyer, but already has tools and programmes. Avoid making a university-wide licence the prerequisite for learning whether users want this.

## Segment choice: split by situation, not just age

The ratings in this table are strategic hypotheses, not measured market scores.

| Segment | Concrete job to be done | Plausible trigger and return pattern | Payment / acquisition hypothesis | Principal risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Singapore tertiary students, 18+, preparing for internships, project presentations or first jobs | Explain a project decision or personal example; respond when questioned; contribute in a discussion | A presentation/interview within 2–4 weeks; short concentrated practice, possibly a longer habit if discussion practice becomes useful | Reach through societies, peer mentors, alumni and workshops. Test small one-off access before subscription. Student or sponsor may pay. | Free campus AI practice and coaching; exam peaks; temporary need; participation because a facilitator asked rather than independent demand |
| Singapore early-career adults, first 0–3 years in work | Explain a recommendation, give a concise update, challenge a proposal respectfully or answer follow-up questions | A recurring meeting/project cycle, not solely the next interview | Individual payment may be more plausible than in the student group, but must be observed. Professional/alumni communities or coach referrals first; manager sponsorship later | General-purpose AI, employer training, limited time; privacy of employer material; app prompts may feel too academic |
| Singapore jobseekers with an immediate interview | Turn real experience into a clear, defensible answer | High urgency until the interview; episodic repeat when the next opportunity appears | A time-limited practice pack is a better initial commercial hypothesis than annual retention | Most direct overlap with campus VMock and dedicated interview coaches; urgency is not a unique feature |
| Global young professionals | Similar communication situations, but different job markets, language needs, currencies and norms | Potentially frequent, difficult to define as one cohort | Later expansion into one role/community/geography after proving a local loop | “Global” is not a usable acquisition segment; more support, localisation and distribution complexity before product-market evidence |
| Curious readers | Move from consuming an article to forming a thoughtful position and discussing it | Ongoing curiosity or book-club rhythm; no necessary deadline | A community extension, retention mode or later reader-club partnership | Large free content supply, no demonstrated need to pay for reflection, and friction from writing after reading |

**First recruitment criterion:** the person can name a recent or upcoming situation in which expressing their thinking matters. Do not recruit only “people who like self-improvement” or “people interested in AI”. Include people already satisfied with existing tools; they are the right test of incremental value.

Within the student cohort, deliberately include polytechnic/ITE/other tertiary pathways where participants are adults, not only university societies. This is a recruitment design recommendation, not an assertion that those groups have worse communication skills. Record institution, course/work context, existing resources, English comfort and access needs without treating nationality or accent as a deficit.

## Existing provision is the relevant competitive baseline

1. **Campus AI interview practice is already available.** NUS explicitly links free access to VMock Interview and describes personalised video-response feedback, available around the clock. [NUS CFG](https://www.nus.edu.sg/cfg/students/career-resources/ace-your-job-interview). NTU describes VMock alongside a career course and small-group mock-interview clinics. [NTU career provision](https://www.ntu.edu.sg/education/career-guidance-industry-collaborations/for-students/career-coaching---consultation). SMU publishes a SMART Interview guide. [SMU guide](https://careerservices.smu.edu.sg/sites/careerservices.smu.edu.sg/files/2025-12/171225_SMART%20Interview%20Guide_Singapore%20Management%20University.pdf). Availability is not evidence of usage, satisfaction or outcomes; ask which tools participants can actually access and have tried.
2. **Human practice offers something the app should complement.** Toastmasters' official directory lists Singapore clubs open to interested guests, with no expectation that guests speak before they feel ready. [Bedok club listing](https://www.toastmasters.org/Find-a-Club/1017-1017). Live attention, relationships and a real audience are not equivalent to automated feedback. A between-meeting rehearsal tool is a hypothesis to test with organisers, not an assumed partnership.
3. **Reading already has communities.** NLB lists multilingual book clubs, adult reading/discussion groups and oral-storytelling activities. [NLB book clubs](https://readingnation.nlb.gov.sg/initiatives/book-clubs/). The app could support preparation or reflection around a meeting; replacing a community with solo AI text would be a different proposition.
4. **General-purpose AI and doing nothing are comparison options.** During research, have participants demonstrate their actual existing workflow, including notes, phone recordings, a friend or a chatbot they already use. Do not compare only against expensive coaches, or assume people currently spend money on the problem.

**Proposed differentiation to test:** a short, repeatable sequence that preserves the learner's original attempt, asks useful questions instead of immediately replacing their words, connects a claim to support, makes revision visible, and offers an optional real friend response. The optional AI-written draft must remain clearly identified. A human-owned thinking process is more distinctive than “AI-generated feedback”, but it is not automatically a moat; the evidence must come from adoption, useful revisions and distribution relationships.

## Launch channels and likely buyers

These are proposed routes and buyer hypotheses. No institution, club or employer has agreed to participate or buy.

| Route | First contact / participant | Concrete pilot offer | Proof needed before asking for more | Hurdles to discover |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Student society, faculty community or peer mentoring group | Organiser with a presentation/interview preparation event | A brief live kickoff followed by 14 days of optional solo practice and a paired conversation | Attendance-to-first-practice conversion; unprompted returns; participants choosing it again after seeing their free alternatives | Organiser capacity, event calendars, group self-selection, whether social pressure creates artificial use |
| Alumni / internship / early-career community | Community lead or an independent career coach | A small cohort preparing one real upcoming work conversation | Repeated use before separate real events; payment from participants or a genuinely budget-owning sponsor | Community trust, unclear buyer, coach concerns about inaccurate advice or losing the human relationship |
| Campus career centre | Careers practitioner first; procurement/budget owner only after discovery | Between-clinic practice for one voluntary cohort, with a concise outcome report | Incremental value over current VMock and coaching, accessible workflows, credible human-reviewed examples | Existing contracts, security/privacy review, accessibility, support burden, integration requests, evaluation standards; confirm locally rather than assume a sales-cycle length |
| Small employer or graduate-programme manager | Manager of a real early-career cohort | Practice concise project recommendations with synthetic/non-confidential scenarios | Independent judgement of usefulness in work, repeat use, manageable facilitation time | Cost, time and proof of work relevance. SBF's findings support these questions; they do not guarantee demand |
| Toastmasters / reading-community organiser | Club education/programme lead | Voluntary preparation between meetings | Members arrive more prepared and still value the live meeting | Permission, fit with programme ethos, extra administrative work; avoid claiming official affiliation |
| Short-form content / search / targeted community posts | Individual with a near-term event | Show an actual first answer → thinking question → user revision, then invite practice | Qualified signup and practice completion, not views/likes | Generic self-improvement attention may not convert; ask community moderators before promotion; broad paid acquisition too early |

**Recommended order:** one accessible student/community pilot and one early-career pilot → independent repeat use → paid continuation → small sponsored cohort → only then broader campus/employer licensing. Use a warm introduction where available, but do not mistake a founder's network for a scalable channel.

**SkillsFuture is not a default checkout option.** SSG describes credit for Singaporeans aged 25+ against eligible training, and publishes separate terms for eligible learning subscriptions. Most 18–24 students are outside that credit eligibility; an app subscription is not automatically approved. Confirm any eligibility before advertising it. [SSG individual initiatives](https://www.skillsfuture.gov.sg/initiatives/individuals), [eligible-subscription terms](https://www.skillsfuture.gov.sg/docs/default-source/resources/terms-conditions/terms-and-conditions-for-sfc-eligible-learning-subscriptions-%28for-applicants%29_vf.pdf).

## Willingness to pay: what is known and what must be tested

No directly applicable primary study found in this research establishes how much Singapore students or early-career adults would pay for **this** response-practice product. Employer demand, graduate salary, funding programmes and student AI usage cannot answer that question. Competitor prices demonstrate offers, not purchases at those prices. Institution-supplied access may suppress individual willingness to pay even when users value practice.

Test three distinct commercial hypotheses rather than blending them:

- **B2C event pack:** limited-period practice for an upcoming presentation or interview. This allows episodic value without pretending the user needs a permanent subscription.
- **B2C ongoing practice:** optional monthly access for people who return across separate situations after their original deadline. Test only after observing that behaviour.
- **Sponsored cohort:** a coach, community or manager buys a defined cohort outcome and facilitation support. This is a different product cost structure and sales motion; participant enthusiasm is not a buyer commitment.

An initial experiment could present real, clearly scoped S$8 and S$16 two-week app-only offers to separate small cohorts after a useful trial. These are **research price points chosen to create a meaningful decision**, not recommended final pricing or market-derived WTP estimates. Keep benefits and usage limits identical, explain the offer honestly, take payment only for access actually delivered, and record refunds. Avoid pretending that an interest button is a purchase or mixing a heavily facilitated workshop into an “app-only” price test. A paid continuation from a stranger is stronger evidence than an encouraging answer from a friend.

For B2B, ask for a paid pilot, named budget owner, intended cohort/date and success criteria. A letter of interest or enthusiastic meeting is weaker evidence. Separate app contribution from human facilitation cost. Calculate contribution after provider usage, storage, payment fees, support and refunds; measure those costs from the pilot before setting unlimited access. Do not extrapolate annual recurring revenue from a one-off workshop.

## Retention and commercial benchmarks: useful caution, not targets

RevenueCat's 2026 report targets 2025 performance and includes more than 115,000 RevenueCat-integrated apps with active subscription revenue and minimum volume thresholds. Its AI comparison reports median 12-month retention of 6.1% for monthly AI subscriptions versus 9.5% for non-AI; annual figures are 21.1% versus 30.7%. These are **subscription-level paid retention**, not active-user retention, and are observational comparisons across heterogeneous apps. Selection, category and plan differences matter. They do not predict a Singapore educational app's retention or prove AI causes churn. [RevenueCat original report and methodology](https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps).

Commercial inference: an AI label can attract attention without establishing lasting value. Keep the garden enjoyable, but judge return through another meaningful response/revision or preparation for another real event. Do not optimise token consumption, points collected, notification clicks or AI drafts generated as if they were learning.

For the reader alternative, Reuters Institute's 2026 report finds 42% sometimes/often avoid news across surveyed markets, while 17% paid for online news in its 20-market pay basket. These figures concern news consumption/payment, **not this app or Singapore alone**. Its main survey used YouGov online non-probability quota samples, January–February 2026, with some repolls; results primarily represent online populations. [Findings](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/dnr-executive-summary), [methodology](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2026/methodology). This is a reason to recruit an already engaged reader subgroup rather than assume broad news interest converts into paid reflection.

## Validation plan: six weeks, no fake TAM

The following sample sizes and thresholds are proposed operating decisions, not scientific power calculations or industry benchmarks. Keep cohort results separate and show numerators/denominators. A small pilot can reject an obviously weak proposition, not establish population prevalence or a causal learning effect.

### Week 1: discovery and substitution

Interview 12 adult students and 12 early-career adults, each with a named recent/upcoming communication situation. Include current users and nonusers of campus tools; recruit beyond friends. Ask them to show the last preparation workflow and what it cost in money/time. Ask what actually happened when challenged, without leading them into claiming poor confidence. Then interview 4–6 organisers/career practitioners/managers about existing provision, participation, purchasing and privacy concerns.

Useful questions:

- “What did you do before the last presentation/interview/meeting? Show me the steps.”
- “What did you already have access to? Why did you use or ignore it?”
- “When did you last ask another person for feedback? What was useful or difficult?”
- “What would you stop doing if you used this? What would still need a person?”
- For a buyer: “Which existing programme or budget would this fit, and what evidence would make you pay for another cohort?”

Choose the narrowest repeated situation that people already expend effort on. A situation experienced once in a year may support a pack, not a weekly habit.

### Weeks 2–3: two separate 15–20-person usability/return cohorts

Give participants one real situation and a useful first practice. Observe an original response, guide use, revision and optional friend turn. Do not force a daily streak or require garden use. Have participants also use their actual available alternative on a comparable task; alternate order to reduce novelty/order effects. Campus tools may cover only interview tasks, so compare within their relevant scope and record mismatches rather than forcing an unfair benchmark.

Track:

1. **Activation:** completed first original response plus a meaningful feedback/guide interaction; separately, proportion making a self-authored revision.
2. **Independent return:** a second practice on a different day without personal facilitator chasing; report reminder use separately.
3. **Situation transfer:** attempted an explanation or response in a real meeting/class/interview, with a short account of what changed. This is self-report, not proof of skill improvement.
4. **Incremental preference:** chooses the app for the next suitable task after trying the alternative, with a specific reason.
5. **Trust/friction:** misleading feedback, transcript errors, accidental AI authorship confusion, microphone/privacy concerns, inability to understand a suggestion, and time to useful revision.
6. **Peer mechanics:** invited friend → joined → both contributed → another session. Distinguish friendship availability from solo product value.

A proposed proceed gate per cohort is at least 60% activation and 40% independent return by day 14, with repeated concrete reports of value beyond the alternative. These thresholds merely prevent moving the goalposts. If return requires repeated personal reminders or most usage is reward collection/AI answer generation, revise the proposition rather than claim habit formation. Report raw counts and uncertainty; do not pool the two cohorts to conceal a weak segment.

### Weeks 4–6: real commitment and repeat use

Offer the app-only paid continuation to willing participants and additional recruits with the same problem. Track payment among all eligible offers, refund/cancellation, subsequent practice and contribution cost. Keep subsidised/free participants out of paid-conversion denominators. A provisional decision gate could be at least five independent paying continuations in the stronger segment plus useful repeat use, before investing in paid acquisition. Five purchasers are a discovery signal, not established price elasticity or product-market fit.

In parallel, seek one paid, scheduled, small sponsored pilot with explicit scope. If students engage but do not pay, test sponsorship **without** declaring B2B validated. If early-career users pay for an upcoming event but stop afterward, consider the event-pack model successful on its own terms rather than inflating subscription expectations.

For actual learning claims, a later study needs task-appropriate human ratings, unseen transfer tasks, blinded raters and a comparator. AI scores or self-reported confidence alone cannot validate effectiveness. Use the separate assessment research already in this repository when designing that work; the market pilot should not advertise scientifically validated skill improvement.

## Unresolved questions / conditions that would change the recommendation

- Can the team recruit beyond a single university or friendship circle without paid ads? If not, the initial channel may be the constraint rather than product quality.
- Do people want a quiet solo practice tool, live social accountability, or a human coach? Friend debate adds coordination; it is not necessarily a universally useful activation step.
- Is the garden pleasant and adult-appropriate for the chosen group, or does it weaken a career-use promise? Test presentation, not only stated preference.
- Do students see guided questions as help with thinking or as extra work compared with requesting a polished AI answer?
- Does feedback help with the actual situation while staying accurate about what can be inferred from a short transcript? Accent, delivery, job readiness and general ability must not be inferred from unsupported evidence.
- Does a private first attempt plus visible self-authored revision produce distinctive value after general-purpose AI tools are offered the same task?
- Are there sufficient repeat situations after the first interview/presentation to support a subscription, or should pricing remain episodic?
- Which existing privacy, accessibility and approval requirements apply to a specific institutional pilot? Establish them with the buyer rather than promising approval or data access that does not exist.

Do not publish a top-down TAM by multiplying students, job vacancies, AI users or readers by a guessed subscription price. First build a bottom-up reachable-cohort model: organisers actually willing to invite participants × eligible participants reached × observed activation × observed paid conversion × observed net contribution. Until those inputs exist, they are explicit scenario assumptions, not market size.
